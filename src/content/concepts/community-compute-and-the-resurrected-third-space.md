What you're looking at is the concept hub for 'Community Compute and the Resurrected Third Space.' This vision revolves around building cooperative data centers and makerspaces that revive communal computing and manufacturing facilities within dead malls or other underutilized urban areas.

**Goal:**
The goal here is straightforward: create community-owned compute resources and modular manufacturing hubs that are financially sustainable, accessible, and beneficial to local residents. These spaces should serve as third places—neither home nor work—that foster innovation, education, and collaboration in a way that traditional commercial enterprises often fail to do.

The narrative of this project arc begins with **'projects/community-data-center-vision,'** where I lay out the foundational idea for these cooperatives. The concept hinges on transforming idle retail space into centers for computation and production, offering both economic opportunities and communal benefits. This vision extends beyond mere physical infrastructure; it's about creating ecosystems where people can learn, share knowledge, and build together without being beholden to corporate interests or proprietary technologies.

Next comes **'2026-09-19'**, which details some practical steps towards realizing this vision in a specific timeframe. Here, we see how community engagement, local partnerships, and open-source hardware/software play crucial roles in making such projects viable. By leveraging existing resources like Free Geek and FabLab models, these initiatives aim to bridge the digital divide while promoting environmental sustainability through recycling and upcycling of materials.

In '**projects/precious-plastics-usa,'** I explore another aspect of modular manufacturing—specifically focusing on Precious Plastics USA's mission to place machines capable of transforming waste into usable products across every major city. This initiative not only addresses pressing environmental concerns but also provides communities with tools for self-sufficiency and economic empowerment. The goal is clear: democratize access to advanced technologies so that anyone can participate in creating value from local resources.

The 'garden/podcast-script' further elaborates on how these ideas fit together, presenting a blueprint for building lives around meaningful work rather than mere survival or exploitation. It emphasizes the importance of transparency ('glass box') governance models where decisions are made openly by all stakeholders involved.

**Current State:**
The current state involves refining business plans (like '**projects/makerspace-nonprofit-partnership-pro-forma**'), developing open-source projects (**projects/i-have-found-a-highly-relevant-and-analogous-open-hardware**) and exploring alternative revenue streams such as renting out surplus capacity in an RV setup (**business-plan-renting-out-this-rv-same-idea-as**). Each step brings us closer to realizing a networked system of community-owned compute resources that can support local innovation, education, and economic development.

What remains unresolved is the governance problem. While there are precedents like Mondragon Cooperatives, finding a model that truly works for these specific contexts—where technology plays a central role—is still elusive. How do we ensure equitable distribution of benefits among all members? What mechanisms prevent capture by external interests or internal factions?

In summary, 'Community Compute and the Resurrected Third Space' envisions cooperative data centers and makerspaces as vital components of resilient communities, fostering both technological literacy and environmental stewardship.
