### 🎙️ Podcast Script: "The Blueprint — JD's Plan to Build a Life That Works"

**Format:** Interview-style conversation
**Host:** Alex (warm, curious, asks the questions family would ask)
**Guest:** JD (the dreamer, builder, and planner)
**Runtime:** ~45–55 minutes
**Purpose:** Give family an easily digestible explanation of JD's plans, revenue streams, and path to independence

#### COLD OPEN

**[MUSIC: Warm, optimistic lo-fi instrumental fades in, plays under narration]**

**ALEX (V.O.):**
What does it look like when someone refuses to fit into a mold that was never made for them — and decides to build their own instead?

Today I'm sitting down with JD — a self-taught technologist, hardware tinkerer, and product builder who has spent the last year quietly designing not one, not two, but an entire ecosystem of business ideas, Kickstarter campaigns, and revenue streams. Some of them involve open-source hardware. Some involve boats. One of them involves mushrooms. All of them share a common thread: they're built by someone whose brain works differently, and who's decided that's not a bug — it's a feature.

This conversation is for his family. It's for anyone who's ever heard "just get a job" and felt their chest tighten. And honestly? By the end of it, I think you'll see what I see: someone who's been working harder than most people realize.

**[MUSIC fades out]**

#### SEGMENT 1: THE STARTING POINT — WHY NOT "JUST GET A JOB"

**[~5 minutes]**

**ALEX:****
JD, welcome. Let's start with the elephant in the room. I know your family worries about you. They want you to be okay. And for a lot of people, "okay" means a steady paycheck and a lease somewhere. So before we get into all these plans — why not just do that?

**JD:****
Yeah. And I want to be really clear — I get it. I understand why they worry. If I were watching someone I love struggle the way I have, I'd probably say the same thing: "Just get a job. Get stable. Figure the rest out later."

But here's what they don't see from the outside: I've tried. Multiple times. And it's not that I can't *do* the work — it's that traditional employment environments burn me out in a way that's not sustainable. My brain is wired differently. I have ADHD. The structure that helps most people feel secure — the routine, the cubicle, the nine-to-five — that structure makes me feel like I'm suffocating. I've spent years thinking I was broken because I couldn't make it work.

There was a point — and I've been honest about this with my mom — where I genuinely thought I wouldn't make it to thirty. Not because of anything dramatic. Just because a lifetime of forcing myself into a shape I don't fit felt impossible.

**ALEX:****
That's heavy. And I appreciate you being honest about it, because I think a lot of people feel that way and never say it out loud.

**JD:****
Right. And the reason I'm saying it here is because I want my family to understand: the alternative isn't laziness. It's survival. I'm not refusing to work — I'm refusing to keep doing the thing that was killing me. What I've been doing instead, quietly, for the last year, is building. Building actual products, actual business plans, actual revenue models. And I think once people see what's actually on the table, the conversation changes from "when are you going to get a real job?" to "okay, which of these do we launch first?"

#### SEGMENT 2: WHY NOT SIOUX FALLS?

**[~5 minutes]**

**ALEX:****
So let's talk geography. You're in Sioux Falls, South Dakota right now. And a big part of your plan involves leaving. Why can't you build this stuff where you are?

**JD:****
A few reasons, and they're all practical — not emotional.

First: **the makerspace and hardware ecosystem doesn't exist here.** I've done a full state-by-state analysis — I literally scored every state on funding access, membership potential, and cost feasibility. South Dakota doesn't even crack the top twenty. The grant pools that fund this kind of work — EDA grants up to ten million dollars, USDA rural business development grants up to five hundred thousand dollars, Appalachian Regional Commission funding — those programs are concentrated in states like Ohio, Michigan, Pennsylvania, New York. Sioux Falls doesn't have the density of makers, the institutional partnerships, or the grant infrastructure I need.

Second: **the community I'm trying to build doesn't exist here yet.** The people I need to find — other builders, hackers, open-source hardware people, people who see technology as a tool for community empowerment rather than corporate extraction — they're concentrated in places like Columbus, Detroit, Pittsburgh, Austin, the Research Triangle. I need to be where those people already are.

Third: **cost of entry matters.** Ohio — specifically Columbus — ranked number one in my analysis. The real estate is cheap. The state has over two point three billion dollars in innovation funding through the Third Frontier program. JobsOhio actively funds workforce development. And the makerspace saturation is still low enough that there's room to build something new.

**ALEX:****
So it's not that you hate Sioux Falls.

**JD:****
Not at all. It's that the business I want to build requires infrastructure that Sioux Falls doesn't have. If I wanted to open a restaurant, I could do it here. But I want to build a makerspace and launch hardware products on Kickstarter — and for that, I need to be somewhere with grant programs, community colleges willing to partner, and a population base that can sustain one hundred seventy paying members.

#### SEGMENT 3: THE KICKSTARTER CAMPAIGNS — REAL PRODUCTS, REAL NUMBERS

**[~15 minutes]**

**ALEX:****
Okay. Let's get into the meat of it. You've got multiple Kickstarter campaigns planned. Walk me through them — what they are, what they cost to build, and what they could realistically make.

##### Campaign 1: BilgeGuard — The Subscription-Free Boat Monitor

**JD:****
The first one — and the one I'd launch first — is called **BilgeGuard**. It's a cellular boat monitor. Simple concept: it's a small box you put on your boat that texts you if your bilge pump activates, if your battery is dying, or if someone moves your boat. And here's the kicker — no monthly subscription.

**ALEX:****
That's the hook?

**JD:****
That's the entire hook. Right now, if you want a boat monitor, you're paying Siren Marine seven hundred and ninety-nine dollars upfront *plus* eighteen dollars a month. Or BRNKL for three hundred to five hundred dollars plus twenty a month. BilgeGuard costs a hundred and ninety-nine dollars, you pop in a two-dollar-a-month IoT SIM card, and you're done. No subscription. No locked ecosystem. Open source.

**ALEX:****
What's it cost to make?

**JD:****
The bill of materials is about thirty-five dollars per unit. Landed cost including assembly, testing, packaging, and shipping is about sixty-five dollars. At a hundred and ninety-nine retail, that's a **sixty-nine percent gross margin** — a hundred and thirty-four dollars profit per unit. Even on Kickstarter with the platform fees, I'm looking at about sixty percent net margin.

**ALEX:****
And the Kickstarter goal?

**JD:****
Fifty thousand dollars. Deliberately conservative — that's only two hundred fifty-one units at the standard pledge tier. I hit break-even at three hundred twenty-nine units. The money goes to: first production run of five hundred PCBs at seventeen thousand five hundred, FCC certification at eight thousand, enclosure tooling at five thousand, fulfillment, video production, and working capital.

**ALEX:****
What's the upside if it takes off?

**JD:****
I've modeled out five years conservatively. Year one: twenty-five hundred units, just under five hundred thousand in revenue, a hundred and sixty-three thousand in EBITDA. Year two: eight thousand units, one point six million. Year three: eighteen thousand units, three point five million. By year five — sixty thousand units, nearly twelve million in revenue with fifty-five percent EBITDA margins. And that's the *conservative* model. No external funding. Entirely bootstrapped from Kickstarter and reinvested profits.

**ALEX:****
Twelve million in five years. From a thirty-five dollar box.

**JD:****
From solving a problem that every boat owner hates. There are eleven point eight million registered boats in the US. Florida alone has one point two million. The global boat monitoring market was valued at six hundred eighty-three million in 2025, projected to hit one point seven billion by 2033. I only need to capture a fraction of a fraction of that.

##### Campaign 2: OpenLooper — The Open-Source Wireless Looper Pedal

**ALEX:****
Okay. Campaign number two?

**JD:****
**OpenLooper** — an open-source wireless looper pedal for musicians. It's a six-track stereo looper built on a Raspberry Pi with an audio interface, controlled by physical footswitches but with a phone or tablet as the display via WiFi. No built-in screen — that's what makes it affordable.

**ALEX:****
Why does removing the screen matter?

**JD:****
It cuts the bill of materials almost in half. The touchscreen version I originally designed cost four hundred twenty to five hundred dollars in parts. The wireless version? Two hundred thirty to three hundred ten. Retail price drops from six hundred-plus to two forty-nine. And the user already owns the display — it's their phone.

**ALEX:****
What do the Kickstarter tiers look like?

**JD:****
Three tiers. **The Software Explorer at nineteen dollars** — just the OS image, web app, and CAD files. That's a hundred percent profit. **The DIY Builder Kit at a hundred forty-nine** — custom PCB, pre-drilled enclosure, programmed controller, LED rings; you supply your own single-board computer and audio interface. And **The Ready-to-Gig Pedal at two hundred forty-nine** — fully assembled, calibrated, tested, ready to plug in.

**ALEX:****
Profit per unit on the top tier?

**JD:****
At bulk production — fifty or more units — the cost is about a hundred twenty per unit. At two forty-nine, that's **a hundred twenty-nine dollars profit per unit, fifty-two percent margin**. Breakeven is only forty backers at the two forty-nine tier. And the product has a built-in community: musicians are fanatical. Every guitar forum, every Reddit thread about loop pedals — these people want an open-source alternative to the Boss RC-500 and the Aeros Loop Studio.

##### Campaign 3: OpenDoor — The Open-Source Privacy Doorbell

**ALEX:****
Number three?

**JD:****
**OpenDoor** — a battery-powered, open-source video doorbell. No cloud subscription. No data harvesting. Local storage on a MicroSD card. End-to-end encrypted remote viewing. Solar option for indefinite power.

The whole pitch is: Ring charges you a subscription to watch your own front door. OpenDoor doesn't. If our company disappears tomorrow, the doorbell still works. If our server goes down, you can run your own. The firmware is open source. The hardware designs are open source.

**ALEX:****
Pricing?

**JD:****
Early bird tier at eighty-nine dollars. The base Standalone unit, the Solar Bundle with an external panel, and the full Local Cloud Ecosystem with the OpenBase station — which is basically a local NVR that replaces the cloud entirely. The ESP32-S3 firmware is already functional — PIR wake, WiFi connect, live video stream, deep sleep cycle, MicroSD recording. We have working prototypes.

**ALEX:****
And the timeline?

**JD:****
Six months from campaign close to backer fulfillment for standalone units. Seven months for the OpenBase ecosystem. Nine months to full retail launch. I've built in a four-week buffer at every production stage because hardware always has delays, and I'd rather be honest about that upfront.

##### Campaign 4: Omarchy OS — The Retro Desktop for Kids

**ALEX:****
This one sounds really different.

**JD:****
It is. **Omarchy OS** is a physical desktop PC — and a companion tablet version — that boots into faithful reproductions of classic operating system interfaces. Think early Windows, early Mac OS, early Linux desktops. But it's not nostalgia for nostalgia's sake. The idea is to give kids — and families — a different kind of screen time. Slower, more intentional, closer to the metal.

**ALEX:****
What do you mean "closer to the metal?"

**JD:****
Modern operating systems hide everything. Files, folders, processes, how programs actually work — it's all behind gestures and infinite scroll. A kid using an iPad has no idea how a computer actually works. You put them in front of a classic desktop and suddenly they can *see* the layers. Files are files. Folders are folders. Programs run and you can watch them run.

It's also an intergenerational bridge. A grandparent who grew up with Windows 95 and a ten-year-old can sit at the same machine and actually talk to each other about computers. They share a mental model.

**ALEX:****
What are the tiers?

**JD:****
Three. The **Desktop tier** — the full Omarchy machine, purpose-built or assembled from accessible parts. The **Tablet tier** — a lightweight build that runs on cheap Android tablets or upcycled devices people already have in a drawer. And the **Resilience tier** — the same box configured as an offline local-first backup: mesh networking, local file server, sneakernet-style content sharing for emergencies or off-grid living.

##### Campaign 5: Hydration Station — Solar Water Dispensers for the Homeless

**ALEX:****
And then there's this one. This is the one that feels the most personal.

**JD:****
**Hydration Station** is a nonprofit building solar-powered, community-maintained water dispensers that give free water to anyone who needs it. No questions asked.

Here's the reality: in 2023, there were twenty-three hundred and twenty-five heat deaths in the United States — the highest on record, a hundred seventeen percent increase since 1999. In Phoenix in 2024, there were a hundred thirteen consecutive days over a hundred degrees. Forty-nine percent of heat deaths in Maricopa County were unhoused people. And the cheapest bottle of water in a gas station is two to three dollars — which is impossible when you have no income.

**ALEX:****
So it's free water, powered by the sun.

**JD:****
Exactly. The Kickstarter goal is fifty thousand dollars. That funds engineering, five pilot machines, software, legal setup for the 501(c)(3), and an operations reserve. The real funding comes after — HUD Community Development Block Grants, EPA Environmental Justice grants — the EPA alone has a two-billion-dollar Community Change Grant program. USDA REAP grants up to a million. The Hilton Foundation, Kresge Foundation. Once we have pilot data and a 501(c)(3), we're eligible for hundreds of thousands to millions in grant funding.

I redesigned the machines to be simpler — my original spec had refrigeration, touchscreens, tap-to-pay, AI cameras. The realistic version is an insulated tank, a button, a usage counter, a low-water alert, and an NSF filter. About twelve hundred dollars per unit instead of five thousand. Same money, ten machines instead of five.

#### SEGMENT 4: BEYOND KICKSTARTER — THE OTHER REVENUE STREAMS

**[~10 minutes]**

**ALEX:****

##### YouTube: AI-Generated Pedal Demo Channel

**JD:****
YouTube channel demoing guitar effects pedals, synthesizers, and drum machines — with AI-generated music parts instead of me playing them. Local AI, no ongoing costs, fine-tuned to match each pedal's sound. Small niche, fanatical audience, direct referral-link income. Phase one: five to ten demos for free, prove the concept, show results. Phase two: sponsors at five hundred to two thousand dollars per video. Phase three: sustainable income.

**ALEX:****
Revenue potential?

##### YouTube: Anti-MLM / Investigative Content

**JD:****
I also have a content series planned around multi-level marketing and billionaire accountability. I built an MLM loss calculator — a web app that quantifies the real cost of being in an MLM: time, relationships, money. The video content uses Remotion — programmatic video generation in React — with animated scenes, data visualizations, and documentary-style storytelling. This is passion work, but viral investigative content on YouTube absolutely monetizes.

##### SkyCue — Autonomous Drone Cinematography for Weddings

**JD:****
This is the ambitious one. SkyCue is an autonomous drone cinematography system for weddings — trigger pods placed along the aisle, indoor UWB positioning, software called CuTTo for flight-path editing, a streaming package called LUMIÈRE. One operator, multiple drone angles, no pilot skill required.

**ALEX:****
Revenue model?

**JD:****
I built a full five-year financial model. Year one: four hundred twenty thousand from the crowdfunding campaign. Year five: five point two million in the base case, up to eleven million optimistic. The US wedding videography market is one point two billion dollars. Premium cinematic packages run five to ten thousand per event. SkyCue hardware plus a sixty-partner network at one to one point two K per rental gets you to a million-dollar run rate pretty fast.

##### KARR Vehicle Security Remediation

**JD:****
This is a cybersecurity service business. Researchers at UCSD found that six aftermarket Bluetooth car security systems — including KARR, which is installed in two point two million vehicles — are vulnerable to replay and exploitation attacks. My business scans cars for these vulnerable devices and removes or patches them.

**ALEX:****
And the economics?

**JD:****
Startup costs: fifteen to thirty-five thousand for tools, a van, insurance, and marketing. Unit economics: a scan costs me five minutes and I charge twenty-five to fifty dollars. A patch takes twenty to thirty minutes, sixty to a hundred dollars. Full removal: one to two hours, one fifty to three hundred dollars. Seventy to eighty percent gross margins. One technician doing four to six jobs a day. Ramp-up: four to six thousand a month in months one through three, ten to fourteen thousand by months four through six, twenty to thirty thousand monthly by the end of year one with two techs and B2B contracts. Break-even at about five thousand monthly in fixed costs — roughly thirty-five to forty jobs.

##### Freelancing: Fiverr and Expert Networks

**JD:****
While all of this ramps up, I'm freelancing. Python automation, Flask web apps, IoT product development consulting, Kickstarter campaign copywriting, AI content pipelines, SEO and market research. I've also signed up for expert networks — GLG, AlphaSights, Guidepoint — where you get paid a hundred to five hundred dollars an hour for consultation calls on your expertise.

##### Precious Plastics USA

**JD:****
Reboot of the Precious Plastics open-source plastic recycling project. Goal: a full set of recycling machines — shredder, injection molder, compression, extrusion — in a makerspace or fab lab in every US city over fifty thousand people. That's roughly eight hundred cities. YouTube as the engine to document, teach, and spread it. This ties directly into the makerspace vision.

##### AI Task Concierge

**JD:****
A subscription product — an AI voice agent that handles anxiety-inducing phone-and-paperwork tasks for ADHD and neurodivergent users. The MVP is a Medical Records Concierge: you give it a list of your doctors, it calls every office, requests your records, monitors the fax and email, follows up if they don't arrive, and organizes everything into your application folder. The tech stack exists — Retell, Vapi, Twilio. The moat is the task-recipe framework and the emotional-load management dashboard.

##### Open-Source Hardware Blog

**JD:****
All of my hardware designs — every Kickstarter product, every open-source schematic — published on a blog. The goal isn't just content marketing. It's to spread market-disruption opportunities so other builders can make them real too. Build the community foundation for a genuine alternative economy, one open-source design at a time.

##### Mushroom Grow Container

**JD:****
An open-source automated mushroom growing system using robotics to handle spore cloning in a totally closed system — no clean room needed. Modular: a cloning unit, an incubation unit, a fruiting unit, and a shared electronics controller. Built from recycled materials wherever possible.

##### Vintage Tool Resale

**JD:****
I've got an inventory of vintage American hand wrenches. I built a tool-price-estimator prototype — you drop photos in, an AI vision model identifies the brand, markings, patent dates, era, condition, and gives you a price range. High-value brands like Snap-on, Plomb, Proto, Billings and Spencer sell for three to ten times common brands. Matched sets in original boxes command premiums. This is short-term cash flow.

##### RV Living — Low Overhead, Mobile Base

**ALEX:****
And the RV?

**JD:****
The RV is central to the whole plan. It's not a business — it's how I keep overhead at zero while I build everything else. No rent. No lease. Mobile. If I need to be in Columbus for the makerspace, I drive there. If I need to be at a trade show, I drive there. An RV is a twelve-to-fifteen-thousand-dollar investment that replaces a twelve-thousand-dollar-a-year rent obligation. In two years, it's paid for itself.

And yes — there's potential to turn it into an RV rental side business later, listing it on Outdoorsy or RVshare when I'm not using it. A decent RV rents for a hundred to two hundred dollars a night. Even at fifty percent occupancy, that's fifteen hundred to three thousand a month in passive income.

**ALEX:****
Plus you designed a solar e-bike-towed camper trailer.

**JD:****
*(laughs)* Yeah, that one's further out. A teardrop camper towed by an Onyx RCR-class e-bike, with dual hub motors on the trailer doing the heavy work at highway speed, solar panels on the roof for campsite power. Budget: fourteen to twenty-six thousand to build. That's the long-term vision — fully mobile, solar-powered, no gas, no rent.

#### SEGMENT 5: THE MAKERSPACE — THE BIG VISION

**[~8 minutes]**

**ALEX:****
All of these individual projects are impressive on their own. But you've got a bigger vision that ties them all together. Tell me about the makerspace.

**JD:****
The makerspace is the hub. Everything else feeds into it and feeds off of it.

The idea: a community-owned fabrication space — wood shop, metal shop, 3D printers, laser cutters, CNC machines, electronics benches, textile equipment — where members pay dues, take classes, and have access to tools they can't afford individually. But it's more than just a shop. It's a **platform for building the open-source alternatives to mainstream technology walled gardens**.

Every product I've described — the BilgeGuard, the OpenLooper, the OpenDoor, Omarchy, the mushroom grower, Precious Plastics machines — they all get prototyped, refined, and produced in this space. Members learn to build them. The community owns the designs. The knowledge spreads.

**ALEX:****
What does the financial model look like?

**JD:****
I've done a full pro-forma. For a five-thousand-square-foot space in Columbus, Ohio:

**Startup costs:** two hundred fifty thousand to four hundred thirty thousand depending on buildout level. The base case is three hundred seventy-two thousand. That's covered by a stack: landlord tenant improvement allowance of fifty to seventy-five thousand, state and local grants via a community college or EDC partner of a hundred to two hundred thousand, community foundation and corporate grants of twenty-five to fifty thousand, founding member crowdfund of twenty thousand, and an SBA-backed credit line of seventy-five to a hundred twenty-five thousand.

**Annual operating costs:** lean year one with volunteers is about a hundred thousand. Staffed year two with a part-time shop manager and program coordinator is about a hundred fifty-four thousand.

**Revenue:** one hundred seventy members at an average of about fifty dollars a month generates roughly a hundred sixty-two thousand a year from dues, day passes, classes, equipment rental, materials sales, and corporate memberships. That's self-sustaining. At two hundred sixty members — the stretch scenario — revenue hits two hundred forty thousand with a twenty-one-thousand-dollar surplus.

**ALEX:****
Is a hundred seventy members realistic?

**JD:****
Columbus metro is two point two million people. A hundred seventy members is zero-point-zero-zero-eight percent of the population. Comparable mature makerspaces in similar metros run two fifty to six hundred members. My target is conservative.

**ALEX:****
And the grants you mentioned — the big ones?

**JD:****
This is why the nonprofit structure matters. A for-profit LLC can't access the biggest pools. But a 501(c)(3) or a partnership with a community college or county Economic Development Corporation can apply for: EDA Public Works grants — five hundred thousand to ten million-plus. USDA Rural Business Development Grants — up to five hundred thousand. State programs like Ohio's Third Frontier with two point three billion in total funding. SSBCI allocations — New York alone got over five hundred million. Virginia's GO Virginia program funds makerspaces directly. My strategy is to start under fiscal sponsorship — costs four to ten percent — and spin out into our own 501(c)(3) once revenue exceeds two fifty to five hundred thousand.

But honestly? I don't think we'll need the fallback. Not because I'm delusional. Because I've done the math. And the math works.

That's not a bunch of random projects. That's an alternative economy. It's building the world I actually want to live in — one open-source design at a time.

And the money part? The money is the proof of concept. It's what lets me keep doing this. It's what lets me tell my family: "I'm okay. I'm more than okay. I'm building something that matters."
