## [Campaign Header Image: A warm, inviting photograph of a parent and child sitting at a small wooden desk, looking at a colorful desktop screen showing an early-2000s folder structure. The lighting is soft and warm—no neon blue lights.]

### The computers we give our kids are black boxes.
They are miracles of engineering, but they are designed to hide their own inner workings. Behind smooth glass and seamless swipes, there are no files. There are no folders. There are no directories, command lines, or visible processes. There is only the App—and the infinite scroll designed to keep them there.

When kids grow up in this ecosystem, they don't learn how a computer works; they learn how to navigate someone else's walled garden. They become highly proficient consumers of technology, but not creators of it.

At the same time, screen time has become a constant household battleground. We try to manage it with lockouts, parental control apps, and timers. But this treats technology as a vice, a punishment, or a chore. 

**We believe there is a better way. We call it Omarchy.**

## What is Omarchy?

Omarchy is a physical desktop computer (and a collection of upcycling kits) designed to be a quiet, slow, and legible alternative to modern tablets and smartphones. 

Instead of booting into a hyper-connected, notification-heavy interface, Omarchy boots into **Omarchy OS**—a custom, lightweight Linux base designed for transparency. Through a parent-managed dashboard, you can boot the machine into faithful, local-only "skins" of classic computing eras:
* **The Era of Files & Folders (Mid-90s style):** Where windows overlap, folders live inside other folders, and files must be explicitly saved to a hard drive.
* **The Early Web Era (Early-2000s style):** A simple browser, local document editors, and basic creative tools.
* **The Open-Source Era (Mid-2000s Linux style):** Exposed terminal windows, customizable widgets, and highly legible system monitors.

**This is not nostalgia for nostalgia’s sake.** It is about digital literacy. By stepping back to eras when operating systems were simple, kids can see the seams. They can see how files interact, how memory is allocated, and how programs actually run.

---

## How It Works Day to Day

1. **The Parent Selects the Era:** Using a physical key-switch or a password-protected admin menu, you set the machine's "era" for the day or week. 
2. **Booting Up:** The machine starts. A simple boot sequence runs on screen, displaying real, readable system messages as the Linux kernel initializes (demystifying the boot process itself).
3. **Calm, Local-First Exploration:** There are no notifications, no cloud sync requirements, and no tracking algorithms. Kids can write stories, draw pixel art, compose basic MIDI tracks, or learn Python in a sandbox environment that doesn't constantly demand their attention.
4. **Shutting Down:** When they are done, they click "Shut Down" and watch the screen go dark. There is a clear beginning, middle, and end to the interaction.

## The Four Tiers of Omarchy

We wanted to make this philosophy accessible, regardless of your space, budget, or the hardware you already own. We have designed four distinct ways to bring Omarchy into your home:

### 1. The Desktop Tier (The Full Experience)
A physical, compact desktop computer built specifically for the home. It features a mechanical keyboard with satisfying tactile feedback, a responsive mouse, and a robust housing built from durable, accessible components. Inside, it runs a dedicated build of Omarchy OS. It’s a permanent fixture for a shared family space—a dedicated place to sit down and create.

### 2. The Tablet Tier (Upcycling the Drawer)
Most of us have an old, slow iPad or Android tablet sitting in a drawer. The Tablet Tier is a lightweight software build and companion setup guide that turns this abandoned hardware into a dedicated Omarchy terminal. By replacing the default home screen with Omarchy OS's environment, you strip away the distraction of modern app stores and turn old glass back into a focused, calm creation tool.

### 3. The TV Tier (The Shared Household Channel)
Modern TVs have become giant app grids, encouraging isolated, individual swiping. The TV Tier turns a television back into a shared household hub. It operates on a simple principle: **mimic old broadcast television.** 

Instead of an infinite on-demand catalog, the TV Tier plays a small, fixed number of local "channels" (themed around educational content, family home videos, public domain cartoons, or local creative projects). 
* **Household Democracy:** If there are multiple kids, channel selection is decided by a simple physical or on-screen voting system. When siblings want to watch different things, the system prompts a vote. The parent retains a physical "override" switch to steer the programming.
* **The Community Channel Board:** You aren't locked into our content. Using a companion portal, families and creators can compile and share their own themed channels. You can subscribe to a channel curated by a science educator, a local history buff, or a family sharing their stop-motion animations. There are no algorithms pushing content based on outrage—just shared tastes, community curation, and unexpected discoveries.

*Note: The TV Tier supports two hardware setups: an SBC (Single-Board Computer) kit designed to plug into older CRT monitors, or a simple HDMI stick build for modern flat screens.*

### 4. The Resilience Tier (The Backup Internet)
Computing shouldn't stop when the power grid or the internet goes down. The Resilience Tier transforms the Omarchy box into a local-first offline server. Using an integrated local wireless mesh, it hosts a full offline library (including curated offline maps, encyclopedia archives, survival manuals, and local communication boards). It is a "sneakernet" and mesh-ready backup system designed to keep your household connected and informed, entirely off the grid.

## Why This Matters: The Intergenerational Bridge

Modern technology isolates us. A child sits with a tablet; a parent sits with a phone. Because the user interfaces are highly optimized and hidden behind proprietary gestures, we no longer share a common language for how we interact with information.

Omarchy creates a bridge. When a parent or grandparent sits down at an Omarchy machine, they recognize the interface. The concepts of files, menus, and desktop icons are shared history. A parent can show a child how they used to organize documents; a grandparent can explain the early web. It turns screen time from an isolating activity into a collaborative, intergenerational conversation.

## Section 4: Risks and Challenges

Every hardware and software project comes with unique hurdles. For Omarchy, our primary challenges fall into four main categories:

### 1. Hardware Sourcing & Supply Chain
Manufacturing and assembling physical computers presents traditional supply-chain risks. 
* **Desktop Units:** We are utilizing a mix of newly manufactured and high-quality, tested refurbished components to reduce waste. While this is more sustainable, it means sourcing relies on component availability from certified refurbishers. 
* **TV Tier (CRT Monitors):** CRT monitors are no longer manufactured. For backers choosing the TV Maker Kit, we do not ship the CRT television itself—only the single-board computer, enclosure, and connection cables. Sourcing a CRT is a local project for the backer, and we will provide sourcing guides. For the components we do ship, single-board computer (SBC) supply chains can occasionally experience unexpected lead-time fluctuations.

### 2. Software Licensing, Intellectual Property, and Trademarks
Our goal is to build faithful desktop "skins" that capture the workflow and user experience of classic digital eras (e.g., overlapping windows, old file managers, retro styling). 
* **IP Review Required:** Recreating the look-and-feel of historic operating systems touches on complex areas of copyright, trade dress, and trademark law. We are not using proprietary code, copyrighted assets, or protected trademarks (such as official historical names or logos) of third-party operating systems. However, cloning visual interfaces requires careful legal scrutiny to avoid infringement claims. 
* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > Before shipping the final software, we must undergo a formal intellectual property review with qualified legal counsel. This review is a prerequisite for our release schedule.

### 3. Community Content Board Moderation & Liability
The TV Tier features a community channel board where users can build, share, and subscribe to custom-themed video channels. Whenever user-generated content (UGC) is shared, particularly in a product designed for families and children, moderation is a major responsibility.
* **Moderation Policy & Infrastructure:** We are proposing a community-driven curation system, but we cannot rely on crowdsourcing alone to ensure safety. We must design and implement a robust content-review policy, clear community guidelines, and reporting infrastructure prior to launching the community portal. 
* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > Before the community board goes live, we must complete a comprehensive legal review of content liability, age-appropriateness compliance, and data privacy regulations (such as COPPA/GDPR-K).

### 4. Software Development & Timeline Realism
We are a small team. While a functional prototype of Omarchy OS is already running on our test hardware, refining the user experience, building out multiple historical era skins, optimizing the upcycling wrapper for older tablets, and ensuring stable mesh networking for the Resilience Tier will take significant development time. Unexpected software bugs, API changes, or hardware compatibility issues could delay our shipping timeline. We promise to communicate openly, publishing monthly updates on our progress and any hurdles we encounter.

## Section 5: Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Q: Isn't this just nostalgia for parents? Why would kids care?
**A:** Nostalgia might be what draws parents to the project, but the experience for kids is about clarity and agency. Modern operating systems hide the filing cabinet, the gears, and the engine. When kids click around an Omarchy interface, they are learning structural concepts: file hierarchies, local directory paths, and active file saving. In our early tests, kids don't find it "old"—they find it fascinating because they can finally see *how* the computer is organizing their files.

---

### Q: Why not just install parental controls or a lock-out app on an iPad?
**A:** Lock-out apps and timers treat screen time as a vice or a resource to be rationed. They don't change the nature of the software. An iPad with a 30-minute timer is still a device designed around infinite scroll, high-frequency notifications, and cloud dependencies. Omarchy isn’t about restricting screen time; it's about providing a *different quality* of screen time—one that is local-first, creative, slow-paced, and physically collaborative.

---

### Q: Is this legal? Are you copying real historical operating systems?
**A:** We are building Omarchy OS on top of a modern Linux base. The "skins" we use are custom, ground-up recreations of the layout, windowing systems, and general aesthetics of classic eras. We do not use proprietary code, copyrighted icons, or trademarked brand names from historical operating systems. That said, user interface design is a complex legal area. As outlined in our Risks section, we are undergoing a formal intellectual property review with legal counsel before release to ensure we are fully compliant with trade dress and copyright laws.

---

### Q: Won't kids get bored of this quickly without access to modern games and app stores?
**A:** **We don’t fully know yet, and this is one of our key areas of discovery.** 
If a child expects the high-dopamine feedback loop of modern mobile games, they might initially find Omarchy slow. However, our goal is to shift the goalpost from passive consumption to active creation. By providing built-in creative tools—like simple pixel art editors, basic text processors, and sandboxed coding environments—we want to engage their natural curiosity. During our pilot phase, we will work closely with backing families to find out which creative tools hold kids' attention long-term, and we will update the OS software accordingly.

---

### Q: Who moderates the community channel board to keep it safe for kids?
**A:** **We are still designing the final system for this.**
What we know for sure is that automated filtering and algorithms are not enough. We want the channel board to feel like a curated community library, not an infinite video feed. We are currently researching a combination of manual verification for creators, parent-whitelisted channels, and community reporting structures. Before we launch this feature, we will publish a clear moderation policy and ensure it undergoes a complete legal review to ensure child safety and privacy.

---

### Q: On the TV Tier, what happens when siblings split the vote?
**A:** The TV Tier has a simple, built-in "democracy engine." When siblings click different channels, the system prompts them to either vote, agree to take turns (which the system can track with a visual queue), or play a simple built-in mini-game (like a digital coin toss or rock-paper-scissors) to resolve the tie. If all else fails, parents have a physical override key-switch on the device (or a password-protected admin app) to settle the dispute. We hope this physical constraint actually helps teach kids negotiation and compromise over shared household screens.

## What is Omarchy?

Omarchy is a physical desktop computer (and a collection of upcycling kits) designed to be a quiet, slow, and legible alternative to modern tablets and smartphones. 

Instead of booting into a hyper-connected, notification-heavy interface, Omarchy boots into **Omarchy OS**—a custom, lightweight Linux base designed for transparency. Through a parent-managed dashboard, you can boot the machine into faithful, local-only "skins" of classic computing eras:
* **The Era of Files & Folders (Mid-90s style):** Where windows overlap, folders live inside other folders, and files must be explicitly saved to a hard drive.
* **The Early Web Era (Early-2000s style):** A simple browser, local document editors, and basic creative tools.
* **The Open-Source Era (Mid-2000s Linux style):** Exposed terminal windows, customizable widgets, and highly legible system monitors.

## Section 5: Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Q: Isn't this just nostalgia for parents? Why would kids care?
**A:** Nostalgia might be what draws parents to the project, but the experience for kids is about clarity and agency. Modern operating systems hide the filing cabinet, the gears, and the engine. **A:** We are building Omarchy OS on top of a modern Linux base. The "skins" we use are custom, ground-up recreations of the layout, windowing systems, and general aesthetics of classic eras.
